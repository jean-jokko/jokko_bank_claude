Verdict

The market is real and the gap Kelen targets still exists, but it is narrower than in 2024 and it is closing. Senegal has about 40,000 formal employers paying 1,540bn FCFA of salaries a year, and roughly two thirds of private wage earners already get paid by phone. Nobody yet combines a business account with its own IBAN, double-signature approvals, payouts to every bank and wallet, cards and SME credit in one subscription. Julaya comes closest on payroll and approvals, Djamo on cards and credit, and Wave owns the payout habit at 1%. Kelen wins only if it is clearly the most complete control product for a finance team, prices below the 1% that SMEs pay today, and reaches companies through accountants and payroll tools rather than through consumer marketing.

Ten findings that matter

  • Julaya is the closest competitor. It is licensed in Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire and already offers payroll and supplier payouts to all banks and wallets (up to 3,000 at once), approval levels, sub-accounts, exports and its own RIB. It has no cards and no credit yet, and it raised 800M FCFA in October 2025 to build SME credit. It charges 0.5–1.5% per payout to wallets, with no subscription.
  • Djamo is the main price threat. Its business account is free and bulk payouts from its web app are free. It also has employee cards, 2 million users and a microfinance licence in Côte d'Ivoire, and is raising a $40M Series C. Its business tools are thin: no documented roles, approvals or sub-accounts, and its API only pays Djamo accounts.
  • Wave sets the price SMEs compare against: 1% per payout, free for the recipient. It has no IBAN, no bank payouts, no approvals and no credit. Wave Bank Africa (20bn FCFA capital) is incorporated in Abidjan but not yet licensed. If it is licensed, Wave becomes the biggest long-term threat.
  • Orange is entering SME banking in Côte d'Ivoire. The OM Business app launched in June 2026 with credit from Orange Bank Africa, which has 3 million clients and a 17bn FCFA SME guarantee line. In Senegal, Orange charges 1% / 1.5% for salary and supplier payments, with settlement in 5 days.
  • Banks are expensive but not absurd. A 10-employee SARL pays about 35,000–52,500 FCFA a month in account, online banking and transfer fees. Opening takes 7–15 working days and a branch visit. No bank offers self-service onboarding with approvals, invoicing and cards at a flat price.
  • PI-SPI, the BCEAO's instant-payment system, is the biggest structural change. It is mandatory from 2 November 2026 and connects 175 banks, wallets and payment institutions, Wave included since 30 September 2026. Through its partner bank, Kelen can reach every wallet and bank at low cost. That makes a flat subscription that includes payouts possible.
  • Payroll in Senegal is already digital; in Côte d'Ivoire it is still cash. About 64% of Senegal's private wage earners are paid by phone, but 57% of Côte d'Ivoire's are paid in cash only. Côte d'Ivoire has more room for payroll digitisation; Senegal has more firms ready to switch provider.
  • Credit is the SME's first problem. In Senegal, 45.9% of firms name access to finance as their biggest obstacle, against a 30.8% Sub-Saharan average. Only 10.8% of small firms use banks to fund investment. Invoice financing is unoccupied, and the SFD lending cap is 24% all-in (TAEG).
  • Senegal's macro picture is weaker than when the plan was drafted. Growth is forecast at about 2.5% in 2026 and the IMF programme is still pending Board approval. The State owes companies 1,956bn FCFA in arrears, and only 300bn is promised by end-2026. Expect slower sales cycles, more late payments and stronger demand for working-capital credit.
  • The plan's Senegal client target looks ambitious. 7,650 active clients in year 5 is 27% of the plan's own target market and 27–38% of the estimated 20,000–28,000 digital-ready Senegalese SMEs. Julaya, after six years, had about 2,800 active users in Senegal in 2024. See the Positioning tab.

Pain points Kelen's product answers

Slow, paper-heavy account opening

RCCM extract, notarised statutes, NINEA, IDs, signature specimens and a branch visit; 7–15 working days to a working card and RIB. A KYB in under 48 hours for registered companies beats every bank.

Fees billed line by line

Banks bill account keeping (8,500–25,000), online banking (5,000–10,000) and 3,000–15,000 per interbank transfer. Bulk salary files cost 1,000 per line. Wallets take 1–1.5% of payroll.

Double signature on paper

Senegalese firms run two-signature mandates. Wave and Orange business tools document no approval workflow; banks do it on paper or in corporate e-banking.

Paying staff on many rails

Employees use Wave, Orange Money, Mixx, banks. Payroll tools (KayFay, Sage, Odoo) produce files but no payment.

Waiting to be paid

The Senegalese State owes companies 1,956bn FCFA (end-March 2025 stock); construction is most exposed. Supplier credit is common.

No working capital

Banks lend at about 7% to the SMEs they accept and require collateral; most small firms are refused and fund themselves.

E-invoicing obligations

Côte d'Ivoire's normalised e-invoice (FNE) is enforced from 1 September 2026, with 52,000 firms registered. Senegal's timetable is still being set.

Spending control

No local spend-management product (a Ramp or Spendesk equivalent) was found; field allowances still move as cash.

Sources: BNDE tariffs 2025 · BIS tariffs 2024 · arrears · FNE · account opening (third-party guide, unverified).

Gaps in the evidence

No survey was found on SME late payments, bank fees or account-opening time. Willingness to pay a monthly subscription has never been measured. Twenty to thirty interviews with Dakar SMEs and accountants would close this before launch.

Landscape

Where every type of player sits, and the empty space Kelen aims for.

How to read it

Every competitor is strong on one axis only. Julaya has the controls a finance team wants but no cards or credit. Djamo, Orange and the banks bring banking depth but little workflow. Wave and the gateways are rails. The top-right corner, a full business account with controls, is empty today.

The corner will not stay empty. Julaya is building credit, Djamo is raising $40M and Wave is waiting for a bank licence. Kelen's launch window is 2027–2028.

Pricing implications for Kelen

  • Include payouts in every plan. A quota of free payouts (for example 30 / 150 / 600 a month) turns the subscription into a saving against Wave's 1%.
  • Sell against the bank, not against Djamo. Petite at 9,900 costs less than a bank's micro-business fee alone (10,000) and far less than the 35,000–52,500 a 10-person SARL pays its bank.
  • Keep a free or near-free entry. Djamo's free account sets the floor for very small firms. A free trial of three months (already in the design) answers it; the paid case is approvals, roles and multi-rail payouts.
  • Invoicing alone is worth 3,900 FCFA a month (Facture SN). It is a feature inside a plan, not a reason to pay 29,900.
  • Check the PI-SPI business tariff with the partner bank before fixing quotas: the two published readings differ.

Positioning

Where Kelen should stand, how competitors will react, and what this means for the business plan.

Kelen's position

The business account for SMEs that pay people. One account with its own IBAN, where the manager prepares and the owner approves, and salaries and suppliers go to any bank or wallet in one run. Cards for staff, invoices for clients and an accountant who sees everything. A flat monthly price that costs less than the bank and less than 1% of payroll.

1. Control, not just payments

Approvals, roles, sub-accounts and accountant access are what Julaya has and Djamo, Wave and Orange lack. Put them in every plan, including Petite.

2. Every rail from one balance

Banks and all wallets through PI-SPI, with Wave and Orange as payout rails, not rivals. Recipients choose where they are paid.

3. Flat price with payouts included

Undercut 1% of payroll and the bank's fee stack. Make the saving visible in the onboarding flow.

4. Payroll in, employees out

Import from KayFay, Sage, Odoo and accountants' files; employees get salary accounts and a salary advance. This is the moat Julaya and Wave do not have.

5. Credit from payment data

Underwrite on payroll and collections history before the SFD (Moniepoint's playbook). Start with invoice and payroll advances, inside the 24% all-in cap.

6. Accountants as the channel

Qonto grew through 6,000 accounting firms. In Senegal many SMEs' payroll is run by accountants: give them free multi-client access.

Risks

  • Wave gets its bank licence and bundles accounts and credit for its whole base.
  • Djamo closes $40M and copies the control features while staying free.
  • Partner-bank dependence: the bank may also serve competitors, change prices or slow integration.
  • Senegal's slowdown: State arrears and weak growth in 2026–27 lengthen sales cycles and raise credit losses.
  • Willingness to pay a monthly subscription has never been measured in this market.

What to verify before launch

  • Mystery-shop Ecobank, SG Sénégal, CBAO and NSIA for their SME tariffs (not published online).

  • Get the PI-SPI tariff for business senders from the partner bank.
  • Track Wave Bank Africa's licence and Djamo's Series C.
