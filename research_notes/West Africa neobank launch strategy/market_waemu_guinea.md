## 1. Macro, demographics and political/security risk

### Takeaway
Côte d'Ivoire is the anchor economy: about US$100bn GDP, about US$3,050 GDP per capita and 6%+ growth with low debt. It is roughly 2.7× Senegal's GDP. Senegal's macro picture deteriorated sharply after the hidden-debt scandal: debt is about 130% of GDP, IMF-projected growth is 2.2% for 2026, and the IMF programme is still pending. Benin and Togo are steady 5–7% growers. Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger (AES) carry acute security and political risk, but all three remain inside UEMOA/BCEAO. Guinea is the fastest-growing economy (8.7% projected for 2026) but sits outside the CFA zone.

**Political and security risk**
- **AES exit from ECOWAS:** Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger's withdrawal from ECOWAS took formal effect on 29 January 2025. As of September 2026 all three still use the CFA franc (XOF) and remain in WAEMU. AES documents propose a joint central bank and currency, but neither has been implemented and no date has been set — [Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/sahel-aes-confederation-economy-2026/); [Rio Times (ECOWAS/CFA)](https://www.riotimesonline.com/west-africa-ecowas-cfa-franc-2026/)
- **AES confederal bank:** BCID-AES was activated on 23 December 2025 with 500bn FCFA (~€762M) of initial capital subscribed by member states. It operates outside BCEAO jurisdiction — [Ecofin Agency](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news-finances/2412-51643-aes-launches-confederal-investment-bank-a-strategic-pivot-toward-sahelian-financial-sovereignty)
- **BCEAO remains the single regulator for all 8 states:** it issues the currency, sets rates and supervises banks through the WAMU Banking Commission — [BCEAO](https://www.bceao.int/en/content/presentation-bceao); [Wikipedia: Banking Commission of WAMU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_Commission_of_the_West_African_Monetary_Union)
- **Mali:** JNIM announced a fuel blockade on 3 September 2025 and has burned 300+ tankers on routes from Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire. Schools closed for two weeks. The blockade eased in early 2026 and was reimposed in late April 2026. As of mid-2026 Bamako still depended on escorted convoys — [Wikipedia: Mali fuel blockade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_fuel_blockade); [OpsCon, Jul-2026](https://theopscon.com/intelligence/threat-level-mali-bamako-fuel-siege-convoys-2026-07-19)
- **Guinea:** Coup leader Mamady Doumbouya won the 28 December 2025 presidential election with 86.72%. More than 50 parties had been dissolved and the main opposition was barred or in exile. A September 2025 referendum allowed officers to run and extended the presidential term to 7 years. He was sworn in on 17 January 2026 — [France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/africa/20251231-guinea-coup-leader-doumbouya-elected-president); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/1/18/military-leader-doumbouya-sworn-in-as-guineas-president); [Wikipedia: 2025 Guinean presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Guinean_presidential_election)
- **Guinea-Bissau:** On 26 November 2025, President Embaló was arrested in a coup the day before results of the 23 November election were due. Gen. Horta Inta-A Na Man was sworn in as transition leader on 27 November 2025. ECOWAS moved to suspend the country. It has had 5 successful coups since 1973 — [Wikipedia: 2025 Guinea-Bissau coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Guinea-Bissau_coup_d'%C3%A9tat)
- **Benin:** A coup attempt in Cotonou on 7 December 2025 (Lt-Col. Pascal Tigri) was foiled within hours by loyalist forces with Nigerian air strikes, and ECOWAS troops were deployed afterwards. The plotters cited insecurity in the north — [Crisis Group](https://www.crisisgroup.org/alr/africa/benin-nigeria/foiled-coup-benin-and-win-ecowas-and-nigeria); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/12/08/nx-s1-5636734/benin-government-regains-control-following-attempted-military-coup)
- **Senegal debt crisis:** The IMF found the 2023 deficit was 12.3% of GDP, not the 4.9% reported. A Court of Auditors audit put end-2023 debt at 99.7% of GDP against 74.4% reported. On 1 September 2026 a staff-level agreement was reached on a 36-month ECF of about US$2.2bn, which is still subject to Board approval, a misreporting waiver and financing assurances. External debt treatment will go through an enhanced G20 Common Framework — [France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20260901-imf-reaches-agreement-with-senegal-on-new-2-2-bn-loan-programme); [Wikipedia: Senegal hidden debt scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegal_hidden_debt_scandal); [Credendo](https://credendo.com/en/knowledge-hub/senegal-debt-restructuring-announced-after-hidden-debt-crisis)

### Inferences
- On a "size × stability" basis Côte d'Ivoire is the clear first market. Senegal is the most digitally mature but faces 2026–27 austerity (tax rises, fiscal compression, slower growth), which will weigh on household disposable income and public-sector payroll growth.
- For the AES states the main risk to a neobank is not losing BCEAO membership in the near term (no date or institution exists). It is operational: security, fuel and power disruptions in Mali, liquidity and NPL stress in Niger, and junta-driven policy unpredictability. A BCEAO-licensed product can legally serve these markets, but go-to-market costs and risk are much higher.
- Benin's 7% growth and low inflation make it the best mid-sized macro story. The December 2025 coup attempt adds some tail risk.

### Gaps
- No median age figures were retrieved (UN WPP). The 0–14 share is used as a proxy: Niger and Mali are the youngest, Senegal the oldest.
- The IMF July 2026 WEO update country figures were not retrieved; April 2026 vintages are used.
- The sanctions history (ECOWAS/UEMOA sanctions on Mali in January 2022, lifted July 2022, and on Niger after July 2023, lifted February 2024, including BCEAO access freezes) is background knowledge that was **not re-verified** in this research. Verify before citing.
- There is no country-by-country political risk index (e.g., World Bank WGI political stability scores). This was not retrieved.

## 2. Financial inclusion, smartphone and internet readiness

### Takeaway
On Findex 2025 (survey year 2024) Senegal is the most "account-ready" market: 76.5% of adults hold an account, 66.9% a mobile money account, 48% a bank/FI account, and 60.8% say their main phone is a smartphone. Côte d'Ivoire, Togo, Mali, Benin and Burkina sit in the 51–58% account band. Guinea (36%) and especially Niger (15%) lag far behind. BCEAO's broader "taux d'inclusion financière" is much higher (73.6% union-wide in 2024) because it counts all registered e-money accounts.

### Inferences
- An **app-first** neobank's realistic near-term addressable base is better proxied by "smartphone as main phone" than by account ownership. Applied to adults (calc.): CI ≈ 8.9M, SN ≈ 7.2M, ML ≈ 6.6M, BF ≈ 5.3M, BJ ≈ 2.5M, TG ≈ 2.3M, GN ≈ 2.4M, NE ≈ 2.7M. Across WAEMU-8 that is roughly 35–36M smartphone-primary adults, plus about 2.4M in Guinea.
- Mali's smartphone share (48.5%) is surprisingly high relative to its income level, but security and fuel constraints limit monetisation.
- The gap between BCEAO's broad inclusion rate (86–90% in BJ/TG/CI/SN) and Findex ownership (52–77%) shows heavy multi-SIM and multi-wallet duplication. Registered wallets overstate unique users.
- Data costs are lowest in CI, TG and SN. Mali and Burkina have expensive data, which matters for app-heavy onboarding (video KYC, selfie liveness).

- Guinea-Bissau has no Findex 2025 data.

## 3. Mobile money: accounts, activity, values and competitive structure

### Takeaway
UEMOA mobile money is huge but shallow. In 2024 there were 248.7M registered accounts but only 76.9M active (30.9%). Transactions totalled 11.9bn, worth 160,415bn FCFA (≈€244.6bn), or 119% of union GDP. Côte d'Ivoire (33.3% of value) and Senegal (30.9%) dominate. Orange still leads union-wide by value (41.3%), but Wave's 1%-fee model took it to 38.2% (2024) and makes Wave the price-setter in Senegal and increasingly in Côte d'Ivoire. E-money issuers as a group are loss-making, which suggests the transfer business is commoditised and the monetisable layer is elsewhere (credit, savings, merchant, payroll).

- **Value per active account** (calc.): Senegal ≈ 3.31M FCFA (€5,046) per year; Mali 3.05M (€4,647); Burkina 2.33M (€3,548); CI 2.11M (€3,222); Benin 1.07M (€1,630); Togo 0.68M (€1,032). **Value per adult**: SN ≈ €6,270, CI ≈ €4,370, BF ≈ €2,170, BJ ≈ €2,110, ML ≈ €2,090, TG ≈ €1,220, NE ≈ €50. Senegal and CI have the deepest wallet flows to build on, both for account-to-account funding and for salary-advance repayment rails.
- Active accounts per adult (calc.) exceed 1 in CI (1.35), SN (1.24), BJ (1.29) and TG (1.19), which confirms multi-wallet behaviour. A neobank does not need to displace a wallet. It can sit on top of wallets as a funding/withdrawal rail, especially with PI-SPI interoperability.
- Merchant acceptance is concentrated in CI (1.17M active merchants), then BJ (280k) and SN (219k). In the Sahel and Togo it is negligible, so a merchant/SME acquiring play is most viable in CI first.
- Niger's 4.5% activity rate and tiny flows (0.3% of union value) make it the least attractive market. The data may also reflect reporting gaps for some operators.
- The fee war has compressed transfer margins (EMEs are loss-making, agents are shrinking and charging informal fees). A neobank should not compete on P2P transfer pricing. Monetisation should come from credit (salary advance, SME working capital), float/savings yield, FX/remittances and B2B services.

## 4. Banking sector structure, credit depth, rates and asset quality

### Takeaway
UEMOA banking is moderately concentrated and profitable: 135 banks, 15.6% ROE and 72,068bn FCFA (€110bn) in total assets in 2024. It is also shallow. Private credit is about 20–31% of GDP in the coastal markets and about 10% in Niger and Guinea, and SMEs pay more than large firms. Senegal (33 credit institutions) and CI (31) are the most crowded and the most competitive (lowest HHI). Niger's banking system is in distress, with a 26.8% gross NPL ratio.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- With 22.2M bank accounts for about 89M WAEMU adults (calc. ≈25%) and private credit at 20–31% of GDP, retail and SME credit is underpenetrated. Formal FI borrowing in Findex is only 3.9% in CI against 17.6% in SN.
- Average household lending rates (about 8.9%) are low by African standards, but access is the binding constraint. A neobank's edge is underwriting thin-file users from wallet and payroll data, not undercutting bank pricing.
- Senegal and CI are the most competitive banking markets (lowest HHI, most banks), but incumbents are branch-heavy. Benin, Togo and Burkina have more concentrated systems, which could leave more room for a digital entrant.
- Niger and Guinea-Bissau's high NPLs, together with Niger's liquidity rationing, argue against lending there early.

- Guinea: number of banks, concentration, NPL and lending rates were not retrieved (BCRG data).
- The BCEAO usury caps were not verified in this research.

## 5. Card usage and e-commerce

### Takeaway
Cards are marginal next to mobile money. There were about 8.0M cards in all of UEMOA at end-2023 (≈9% of adults), 25% of them on the regional GIM-UEMOA scheme, and card value is mostly ATM withdrawals. Debit card ownership is high only in Senegal (32.7%). Online purchasing is nascent: 8–22% of adults have bought online, and most pay cash on delivery.

## 6. Payroll and formal employment (salary advance relevance)

### Takeaway
Wage earners are a minority: 20–31% of adults in CI, SN, TG and BF, and about 10% in BJ, NE and GN. Wages are still largely paid in cash outside Senegal. Senegal is the only market where wages via bank or mobile (12% + 20% of adults) clearly exceed cash wages (7.5%). In CI and Togo, cash-only wage recipients (10%) outnumber those paid into a bank account (4–6%). That makes Senegal the easiest market for salary-linked lending, and CI and TG the biggest "payroll digitisation" opportunities.

In CI the opportunity is converting cash-paid private-sector employees through employer partnerships (B2B payroll product), which also opens the SME side.

- The SME credit gap ($ or % GDP) was not retrieved.

## 8. Remittances and diaspora

### Takeaway
Remittances matter most for Senegal: about 10.6% of GDP (WDI 2023), or 2,211bn FCFA (≈€3.37bn) in 2024. That is about 12% of GDP and more than official development aid. Senegal also captures 64% of international inbound mobile money transfers into UEMOA. Guinea-Bissau (9.9% of GDP) and Togo (8.7%, 2020) are also remittance-dependent. In the other markets remittances are 2–4% of GDP. A March 2026 BCEAO notice lets diaspora members open local CFA accounts remotely on resident terms, which is a direct opening for a neobank.

### Cited Findings
- Personal remittances received (WDI, latest year): SN US$3.27bn (2023, 10.6% GDP); CI US$1.77bn (2024, 2.0%); ML US$1.07bn (2024, 4.0%); NE US$0.65bn (2024, 3.3%); TG US$0.65bn (2020, 8.7%); GN US$0.62bn (2024, 2.5%); BF US$0.60bn (2024, 2.6%); BJ US$0.34bn (2023, 1.7%); GW US$0.22bn (2024, 9.9%) — [WDI BX.TRF.PWKR.CD.DT](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/BX.TRF.PWKR.CD.DT); [WDI BX.TRF.PWKR.DT.GD.ZS](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/BX.TRF.PWKR.DT.GD.ZS)

- Inbound international transfers received into wallets from outside UEMOA (2024): SN 27.4M transactions / 1,700.8bn FCFA (€2.59bn); ML 8.5M / 209.0bn (€0.32bn); CI 5.6M / 480.1bn (€0.73bn); BJ 1.9M / 194.3bn (€0.30bn); BF 0.9M / 64.6bn; NE 0.47M / 0.2bn; TG 0 reported. ### Inferences
- Senegal is the strongest diaspora and remittance market. A combined product (euro inflow → FCFA account → savings, salary advance or housing for diaspora families) fits there first. Mali is second for inbound international transfers.
- The CI–BF–ML intra-UEMOA corridor (≈€6.8bn in 2024, calc.) is a large cross-border flow that a WAEMU-wide licence could serve natively.

## 10. Country-specific regulatory and tax issues

### Takeaway
Senegal introduced the region's most aggressive tax on digital finance in late 2025: 0.5% per transfer (capped at 2,000 FCFA) plus 0.5% withholding on merchant receipts. Côte d'Ivoire taxes operator revenues (7.2% on transfer fees since 2019), not transactions, and from 2026 makes mobile money business payments tax-deductible. Guinea requires a separate BCRG licence and GNF operations with no passporting.

### Cited Findings
- **Senegal:** Law 2025-17 of 27 September 2025 imposes 0.5% per transfer operation, capped at 2,000 FCFA (€3.05), whatever the channel (mobile, bank transfer, card, postal). It adds 0.5% withholding on merchant receipts via electronic platforms. Orange applied it from 16–17 December 2025 to transfers, payments and withdrawals above 20,000 FCFA per 24h. Exempt: cash deposits, withdrawals of 20,000 FCFA or less per 24h, ordinary bank transfers, same-network transfers, state transfers, and salary and scholarship payments. The revenue target is 230bn FCFA (€351M) — [Seneweb](https://www.seneweb.com/en/news/Societe/orange-money-la-taxe-sur-les-transferts-dargent-tta-de-05-entre-en-vigueur-des-le-17-decembre_n_477669.html); [Le Marché Finance](https://lemarche.finance/transferts-dargent-mise-en-vigueur-de-la-tta-a-05-chez-orange-finances-mobiles-senegal/); [Ecofin Agency](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news/0312-51068-senegal-s-tax-on-mobile-money-a-bold-move-with-an-uncertain-payoff); [Jeune Afrique](https://www.jeuneafrique.com/1725572/economie-entreprises/senegal-ce-que-craignent-les-operateurs-de-mobile-money-avec-la-nouvelle-taxation/)
- **Côte d'Ivoire:** the 2019 tax annex imposed 7.2% on mobile money transfer revenue (5% telecom tax, 2% ICT development, 0.2% artistic creation). The DGI said it must be borne by operators, not customers — [Agence Ecofin](https://www.agenceecofin.com/fiscalite/2602-64267-cote-divoire-la-taxe-sur-le-mobile-money-est-desormais-effective); [Agence Ecofin (DGI)](https://www.agenceecofin.com/gouvernance-economique/1502-64051-cote-d-ivoire-selon-la-dgi-la-taxe-sur-le-mobile-money-est-payable-par-les-operateurs-et-non-les-consommateurs). The 0.5% tax on mobile transfers announced in the 2018 budget is reported by some sources as withdrawn and never applied. This is unverified — [Agence Ecofin (2018)](https://www.agenceecofin.com/monetique/1001-53334-cote-d-ivoire-l-etat-impose-une-taxe-de-0-5-sur-le-transfert-d-argent-par-mobile). The 2026 annex makes mobile money payments deductible for businesses — [KPMG CI](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/ci/pdf/newsletter/mensuel-juridique-012026.pdf.coredownload.inline.pdf)
- **Guinea:** e-money issuers must hold client funds at an approved commercial bank (Law L/2017/031/AN on inclusive financial institutions). The BCRG regulates and is building a national switch and phone-number-to-account linkage (2026). Orange and MTN have no direct interoperability — [Mediaguinée](https://mediaguinee.com/2026/04/du-billet-au-digital-au-dela-du-cash-le-gsm-au-service-de-la-circulation-monetaire-par-dr-adama-guilavogui); [Agence Ecofin](https://www.agenceecofin.com/dossier/0907-89967-trois-ans-apres-o-en-est-l-interoperabilite-mobile-money-promise-entre-orange-et-mtn); [UNCDF diagnostic](https://www.uncdf.org/download/file/127/648/pnuduncdfdiagnosticsfnguineerapportfinalpdf)
- **UEMOA-wide:** BCEAO supervises EMEs and banks through the Commission Bancaire, which issued 22 disciplinary and 16 financial sanctions in 2024 — [CB-UMOA 2024 key figures](https://www.bceao.int/sites/default/files/inline-files/Chiffres_cl%C3%A9s_du_Rapport_annuel_2024_de_la_Commission_Bancaire.pdf). The diaspora account notice dates from March 2026, and outbound transfers fall under Regulation 06/2024/CM/UEMOA — [Launch Base Africa](https://launchbaseafrica.com/2026/03/19/bceao-opens-a-new-playbook-for-west-african-fintechs-in-diaspora-banking/)

### Inferences
- Senegal's TTA exempts bank transfers and salary payments. That may tilt users towards bank-account-based products, which favours a licensed neobank over pure wallets, though the 0.5% still applies to card payments and many wallet flows.
- Guinea would need a separate licence, GNF treasury and FX management (GNF is not pegged), and separate interoperability. It cannot share the BCEAO stack, so treat it as a later, standalone market.

### Gaps
- Tax rules on digital financial services in Benin, Togo, Mali, Burkina and Niger (e.g., excise on mobile money fees) were not retrieved.
- Exact BCEAO capital requirements (bank vs EME vs payment institution) and branch passporting procedures were not verified (see section 11).

## 11. The WAEMU-wide licence and regional TAM

### Takeaway
A single BCEAO-regime licence (EME or bank, with country deployment subject to BCEAO/Commission Bancaire procedures) gives access to a single-currency market of about 155M people and 89M adults. That market had 76.9M active wallets, 22.2M bank accounts, 3.7M merchants and about €245bn of annual mobile money flows (2024). It is roughly 6× the adult population of Côte d'Ivoire alone. The sequencing still matters: CI + SN = 64% of mobile money value and 57% of active merchants.

### Cited Findings
- UEMOA 2024 totals: 248.7M e-money accounts, 76.9M active, 11.92bn transactions worth 160,415bn FCFA (€244.6bn), 3.71M registered merchants, 1.59M service points — [BCEAO SFN 2024](https://www.bceao.int/sites/default/files/2026-03/Rapport%20annuel%20sur%20les%20services%20financiers%20num%C3%A9riques%20dans%20l'UEMOA%20-%202024.pdf)
- Côte d'Ivoire and Senegal together account for about 65% of 2024 mobile money value — [Launch Base Africa](https://launchbaseafrica.com/2026/03/24/the-267bn-crown-wave-edges-closer-to-dethroning-orange-in-west-africa/). By the BCEAO annex the figure is 64.2% (33.27% + 30.91%) — [BCEAO SFN 2024](https://www.bceao.int/sites/default/files/2026-03/Rapport%20annuel%20sur%20les%20services%20financiers%20num%C3%A9riques%20dans%20l'UEMOA%20-%202024.pdf)
- 22.2M bank customer accounts across UEMOA (2024) — [CB-UMOA 2024 key figures](https://www.bceao.int/sites/default/files/inline-files/Chiffres_cl%C3%A9s_du_Rapport_annuel_2024_de_la_Commission_Bancaire.pdf)
- E-money issuance is organised per country even within UEMOA: Wave needed a Niger licence or partnership with Ecobank Niger in 2024, and the BCEAO tracks issuers by country — [BCEAO SFN 2024](https://www.bceao.int/sites/default/files/2026-03/Rapport%20annuel%20sur%20les%20services%20financiers%20num%C3%A9riques%20dans%20l'UEMOA%20-%202024.pdf)
- Wave Bank Africa was chartered in Côte d'Ivoire with 20bn FCFA capital — [TriplePundit](https://triplepundit.com/2025/wave-mobile-money-cote-divoire/)

### Inferences
- **Regional TAM (calc.)**, for WAEMU-8 (2025 WDI / 2024 BCEAO):
  - Adults ≈ 89.3M.
  - Smartphone-primary adults (Findex share × adults, excluding GW) ≈ 35M.
  - Adults with any account ≈ 45M (Findex-weighted).
  - Wage earners ≈ 17M.
  - Active merchants ≈ 1.7M.
  - Mobile money flows ≈ €245bn per year.
  - Bank loans outstanding ≈ €56bn.
  - Adding Guinea adds ≈9M adults, about 2.4M smartphone-primary adults and US$28bn GDP, but needs a separate BCRG licence.
- **Serviceable market at launch**: CI + SN together hold about 31M adults (35% of WAEMU), about 16M smartphone-primary adults (≈45% of the WAEMU total), 7.6M wage earners, 1.39M active merchants (81%) and €159bn of mobile money flows (65%). Adding Benin and Togo (coastal, stable, 5–7% growth) brings coverage of smartphone-primary adults to about 60% and of mobile money value to about 75%, with low incremental regulatory cost under one regulator, one currency and PI-SPI interoperability.
- A WAEMU licence lowers marginal market-entry cost mostly to local partnerships (agents, cash-in/out, payroll employers), KYC/ID integration (each country has a different national ID system) and marketing. That argues for a "license once, launch CI/SN, then BJ/TG, keep AES as opportunistic" strategy.
- The licence does not remove country-level operational steps. Banks need per-country establishment or branch authorisation, and EMEs appear to operate per-country initiatives, sometimes via bank partnerships.

### Gaps
- The exact legal mechanism and timeline for cross-border operation of a BCEAO-licensed EME or bank (single licence vs per-country authorisation, branch notification, minimum capital) was not verified in primary BCEAO texts. **Verify with counsel or BCEAO instructions before relying on "one licence = 8 markets".**

Scoring rationale (judgement calls, based on the data above):
- **Côte d'Ivoire:** largest adult base and GDP, 1.17M active merchants, about 4M wage earners with 10% of adults still paid in cash (conversion opportunity), 6%+ growth, moderate debt, a tax regime that taxes operators rather than transactions, and a 2026 rule favouring SME digital payments. Penalised for the most crowded field (19 issuers, Orange, MTN, Moov, Wave, Wave Bank, Djamo, Orange Bank).
- **Senegal:** best digital, account and payroll readiness (60.8% smartphone, 76.5% accounts, 32% of adults paid digitally, 32.7% debit card) and the highest remittances. Penalised for debt of about 130% of GDP, 2.2% growth in 2026, a pending IMF programme, the 0.5% TTA and merchant withholding, and Wave's dominance.
- **Benin:** strong growth and stability, the highest BCEAO inclusion rate (89.8%) and sizeable merchant base (280k). Low smartphone (29%) and wage-earner (10%) shares.
- **Togo:** high activity rate (48%) and good data prices, but a small market with weak merchant mobile money.
- **Mali / Burkina:** decent smartphone and mobile money usage, but acute security risk, AES uncertainty and NPL stress (Mali 12.4%).
- **Guinea:** 8.7% growth and a two-player wallet duopoly without interoperability (whitespace), but a separate BCRG licence, GNF and junta-origin government.
- **Niger:** 5.7% mobile money accounts, 4.5% wallet activity, 26.8% NPL and deflation. Avoid.
- **Guinea-Bissau:** tiny market and November 2025 coup.

Sensitivity (calc.): CI's lead is robust. If macro/political weight is cut to 5% and digital readiness raised to 25%, CI still leads (4.15 vs SN 3.90). Senegal overtakes only if the model ignores macro, tax and competition and scores pure demand: digital 30%, behaviour 30%, payroll 20%, size 20% gives SN 4.6 vs CI 4.4. The CI/SN choice therefore hinges on whether the founder prioritises scale, stability and merchant/SME breadth (CI) or digitally native, salaried and diaspora users despite fiscal and tax headwinds (SN).

They should be filled in a follow-up round. **The web search budget was exhausted during this research**, so CEPICI/APIX business creation statistics, civil-service headcounts and diaspora corridor data could not be searched.
